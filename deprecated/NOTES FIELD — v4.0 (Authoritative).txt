NOTES FIELD — v4.0 (Authoritative)
Notes is a free‑text, unstructured field used to capture contextual, descriptive, or clarifying information that does not belong in any controlled field.
Notes is the only field allowed to contain narrative text.

🌿 1. What Notes is
Notes is the place for:
A. Clarifications
- ambiguous ownership situations
- historical ownership changes
- unusual management arrangements
- partial or shared stewardship
- access nuances (since you don’t want an Access field)
- “site is only accessible via X”
- “site is part of a larger complex”
B. Supporting details
- alternate names
- former names
- internal identifiers
- parcel IDs
- planning document references
- URLs to authoritative sources
- legal descriptions
- boundary clarifications
C. Contextual information
- ecological notes
- cultural notes
- historical notes
- interpretive notes
- restoration notes
- stewardship philosophy
- research notes
D. Exceptions
- “Ownership unknown; county auditor lists no parcel”
- “Management unclear; signage contradicts website”
- “Designation pending approval”
E. Multi‑entity relationships
- “Site lies within the XYZ Local Historic District”
- “Site is part of the ABC Greenway Network”
- “Site contains access to the DEF Trail”
F. Anything that does not belong in a controlled field
Notes is the pressure‑release valve that keeps the rest of the schema clean.

🌿 2. What Notes is not
Notes must not contain:
A. Category, Subtype, Designation, Ownership, Management, Status, Coordination
Those belong in their fields.
B. Access rules that contradict Status
If Status = No Public Entry, Notes must not say “open to the public.”
C. Invented or speculative information
No guesses.
No assumptions.
No “probably.”
D. Personal opinions
No editorializing.
E. Sensitive or private information
No personal names of private landowners.
No phone numbers unless official.

🌿 3. Notes Field Rules (Critical)
Rule 1 — Notes must never contradict controlled fields.
If Category = Park, Notes cannot say “this is a nature preserve.”
Rule 2 — Notes must not be used to fix errors in other fields.
If Ownership is wrong, fix Ownership — don’t explain it in Notes.
Rule 3 — Notes must not contain governance or identity.
No:
- “This is a State Nature Preserve”
- “This is a County Park”
Those belong in Designation or Category.
Rule 4 — Notes may contain narrative context.
This is the only field where narrative is allowed.
Rule 5 — Notes may contain multiple paragraphs.
This is the only field where that is allowed.
Rule 6 — Notes may contain citations or references.
URLs, document names, etc.
Rule 7 — Notes may be blank.
If nothing needs to be said, leave it empty.

🌿 4. Examples of Good Notes
Example 1 — Access nuance
“Site is active but access is limited to guided tours offered monthly by ODNR.”
Example 2 — Boundary clarification
“Site includes parcels 123‑45‑678 and 123‑45‑679; excludes adjacent parcel 123‑45‑680.”
Example 3 — Historical context
“Formerly known as Riverside Park until 1998.”
Example 4 — Multi‑entity relationship
“Site lies within the Downtown Local Historic District.”
Example 5 — Stewardship nuance
“Volunteer stewardship provided by Friends of the Ravines under a formal agreement.”
Example 6 — Ownership ambiguity
“Ownership unclear; county auditor lists parcel as tax‑exempt with no owner name.”

🌿 5. Examples of Bad Notes
❌ “This is a State Nature Preserve.”
(That belongs in Designation.)
❌ “Owned by the City of Columbus.”
(That belongs in Ownership.)
❌ “Managed by Metro Parks.”
(That belongs in Management.)
❌ “Closed for the season.”
(That belongs in Status.)
❌ “Probably owned by ODNR.”
(No speculation.)

🌿 6. Notes Field — Final Summary
Notes is:
- contextual
- narrative
- clarifying
- descriptive
- non‑authoritative
- non‑identity‑bearing
- non‑governance
Notes is NOT:
- Category
- Subtype
- Designation
- Ownership
- Management
- Coordination
- Status
- Access rules
- speculation
- identity

 1. Description = Identity
Description is the narrative identity of the Site.
It answers:
“What is this place, in human terms?”

Description contains:
- a concise narrative of what the Site is
- its defining features
- its purpose or character
- its physical or cultural essence
- what a visitor would understand the Site to be
- interpretive or contextual framing that helps define identity
Description does NOT contain:
- ownership
- management
- designation
- access rules
- governance details
- legal clarifications
- exceptions
- parcel IDs
- multi‑entity relationships
- operational nuance
- speculation
- identifiers
- anything that contradicts controlled fields
Description is identity‑bearing.
It is the only narrative field that contributes to the Site’s conceptual definition.

🌿 2. Notes = Context
Notes is the contextual, clarifying, exception‑handling, nuance‑capturing field.
It answers:
“What else do we need to know that doesn’t define the Site?”

Notes contains:
- clarifications
- exceptions
- boundary notes
- access nuance (since you don’t want an Access field)
- historical names
- former uses
- stewardship partners
- research notes
- citations
- URLs
- parcel IDs
- multi‑entity relationships
- “this site lies within…”
- “this site includes parcels…”
- “ownership unclear…”
- “designation pending…”
Notes does NOT contain:
- identity
- category
- subtype
- designation
- ownership
- management
- status
- governance
- invented or speculative information
Notes is not identity‑bearing.
It is the attic, not the foundation.

🌿 3. The Core Distinction (the rule that keeps everything clean)
Description = What the Site is
Notes = Everything important that the Site is not defined by
This is the philosophical backbone of your ontology.

🌿 4. How they interact (the choreography)
A. Description sets the identity.
Notes must never contradict it.
B. Notes may elaborate on Description, but never redefine it.
If Description says:
“A 45‑acre forested park with trails and a creek.”

Notes may say:
“The park includes two parcels acquired in 1998 and 2004.”
“Access is through the adjacent school property.”
“Formerly known as Maple Woods.”

But Notes may NOT say:
“This is a nature preserve.”
(because that would redefine identity)

C. Description is stable; Notes is flexible.
Description rarely changes.
Notes changes whenever new information emerges.
D. Description is visitor‑facing; Notes is analyst‑facing.
Description is what you’d tell a visitor.
Notes is what you’d tell a GIS analyst, historian, or steward.
E. Description is narrative; Notes is narrative‑plus‑technical.
Notes can contain:
- paragraphs
- citations
- URLs
- parcel numbers
- legal language
Description should remain clean and readable.

🌿 5. Examples (this is where the distinction becomes crystal clear)
Example 1 — A county park with a preserve inside it
Description:
“A 120‑acre county park featuring woodlands, wetlands, and a 1.5‑mile loop trail.”
Notes:
“The western 40 acres are designated as a Local Nature Preserve.
Site includes parcels 123‑45‑678 and 123‑45‑679.
Access to the preserve area is restricted during nesting season.”

Example 2 — A historic cemetery
Description:
“A 19th‑century cemetery containing early settler graves and a restored stone chapel.”
Notes:
“Registered Cemetery under Ohio Revised Code 4767.02.
Formerly known as Old Union Burial Ground.”

Example 3 — A nature preserve with limited access
Description:
“A high‑quality fen ecosystem featuring rare sedges and marl flats.”
Notes:
“Access is by permit only through ODNR.
Site lies within the XYZ Local Historic District.
Research access coordinated with Ohio State University.”

🌿 6. The Golden Rule
If the information defines what the Site is, it belongs in Description.
If the information explains, clarifies, contextualizes, or qualifies the Site, it belongs in Notes.

This rule has held up across every edge case we’ve tested.

🌿 7. Final Summary
Description = Identity
- What the Site is
- Narrative, visitor‑facing
- Stable
- Identity‑bearing
Notes = Context
- What we need to know that doesn’t define identity
- Narrative + technical
- Flexible
- Non‑identity‑bearing
Together, they form a clean, powerful pair that keeps your schema both expressive and disciplined.
Your Description is what a visitor needs to know to understand the place.
Your Notes are what a steward, historian, or analyst needs to know to understand the place.
